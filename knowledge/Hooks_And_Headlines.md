# Hooks and Headlines

**Use this file when:** writing any opener (an ad hook, a headline, a subheadline, a subject line, the first seconds of a video, text on screen), or working out why an opener is not landing.

**Covers:** ten hook types, the specificity ladder, awareness levels, headline and subheadline pairs, video hooks, the language bank, variants, testing, platform safe phrasing, adapting to the market, common mistakes and a preflight checklist.

## A note on the examples

Every example belongs to a made-up business, and every number in an example is invented. In real copy, a number, result or name only appears if it is in `business/05_PROOF.md` or `business/04_OFFER.md`. When the proof a hook needs is missing, write `[ADD PROOF: what is needed]`.

## What a hook and a headline must do

A hook is the first line or first seconds of anything: an ad, a video, a subject line, a post. A headline is the main line on a page or a static ad. Both have one job, which is to earn the next line. A working opener:

1. **Selects the right reader.** The person under "Dream customer" in `02_AUDIENCE.md` feels it is about them, and the wrong person scrolls past.
2. **Gives one reason to keep going.** A pain they recognize, a result they want, a question they need answered, or a claim they disagree with.
3. **Is specific.** A concrete situation, number, time or quote beats a general promise.
4. **Is paid off.** The body or page delivers what the opener sets up, quickly. Otherwise it is clickbait.

A plain line that names the exact problem beats a clever one. If a competitor could run the line unchanged, rewrite it with something only this business can say: "What makes us different" in `01_BUSINESS.md`, a language bank quote, or a result from the proof file.

## Hook type versus angle

- The **hook type** is the form of the line: a question, a list, a story opening.
- The **angle** is the reason to care, labeled Pain, Curiosity, Proof, Contrarian, Identity, Story, Transformation, and Urgency (only for a real deadline or limit).

Variants must differ in angle, not only in form. "3 mistakes that cost contractors money" and "The mistake that costs contractors money" are one angle in two forms. Pick the angle first, then the hook type that carries it best.

## Ten hook types

**1. Pain call-out.** For problem aware readers with a pain they would say out loud. Label: Pain.
Pattern: [the frustration in their words] + [a situation they know].
- "Finding out in April whether last year's jobs made money." (bookkeeping firm)
- "The same roof leak, back after every heavy rain."

Describe the situation, never a trait of the person.

**2. Specific result.** For problem or solution aware readers, when `05_PROOF.md` has a concrete result. Label: Proof.
Pattern: [who] went from [before] to [after] in [time].
- "A 14 person roofing company went from 4 months behind on its books to current in 6 weeks." (bookkeeping firm)
- "One clinic stopped losing Tuesday afternoons to no-shows after moving reminders to text." (dental practice)

For skeptical readers, use a typical result rather than the best one.

**3. Curiosity gap.** For unaware or problem aware readers, cold audiences, organic content. Label: Curiosity.
Pattern: [a surprising point] + [the how held back].
- "The one question we ask before quoting any roof."
- "The line on an invoice that makes clients pay late." (invoicing software)

Pay it off within the next few lines. Never fake mystery.

**4. Contrarian claim.** For solution aware readers who tried the usual fixes, when the business genuinely works differently. Label: Contrarian.
Pattern: [common belief] + [why it falls short] + [a hint of what works].
- "More workouts won't fix a week that keeps falling apart. Shorter ones might." (online fitness coach)
- "A general bookkeeper can't tell you which jobs lost money. That isn't their job."

It must be defensible and genuinely held. Never name a competitor.

**5. Identity call-out.** For narrow niches where the reader identifies with a role, trade or business type. Label: Identity.
Pattern: "For [specific role or business type] who [situation]."
- "For roofing and siding companies with 10 to 30 people on the crew." (bookkeeping firm)
- "For dentists who own their practice and still build the schedule themselves." (practice software)

Call out a role or a situation the reader chose, never a personal attribute. "For new practice owners" passes. "For dentists over 50" fails.

**6. Story opening.** For unaware readers, founder videos, emails. Needs a real story from "Origin story" in `01_BUSINESS.md` or from `05_PROOF.md`. Label: Story.
Pattern: [a person] + [a moment] + [tension], starting mid-scene.
- "My dad almost lost his roofing company in the busiest year he ever had." (bookkeeping firm)
- "The call came at 6 in the morning, from a kitchen with water across the floor." (plumber)

**7. Mistake warning.** For readers trying to solve the problem themselves. Label: Pain if it leads with the cost, Curiosity if it leads with the unknown.
Pattern: "The [mistake] that [consequence]." or "Don't [action] until [check]."
- "Don't sign a roofing quote until you find this line."
- "The scheduling habit that leaves gaps in a dental calendar." (practice software)

The mistake must be real for this audience. Inform, never shame.

**8. How to promise.** For problem and solution aware readers, lead magnets, webinars. Label: Transformation.
Pattern: "How to [specific outcome] without [the thing they dread]."
- "How to see profit per job every month without doing the books yourself."
- "How to train three times a week when your calendar changes every week." (online fitness coach)

Take the "without" from "What holds them back" or "What they have tried" in `02_AUDIENCE.md`.

**9. Number list.** For cold audiences, content, lead magnets. Label: Curiosity, or Pain for a list of mistakes.
Pattern: "[number] [specific things] [for a specific reader or result]."
- "4 questions to ask before you hire a roofer."
- "3 reports to read before you bid your next job." (bookkeeping firm)

The number is the real count, and the content delivers exactly that many.

**10. Direct offer.** For product aware and most aware readers, retargeting, a real promotion. Label: the reason it leans on, usually Proof or Transformation.
Pattern: [the offer] + [the main benefit] + [the next step].
- "Book a free 20 minute books review. We look at your last 3 months and show you where the money went."
- "New patient cleaning and checkup for [price from 04_OFFER.md]. Evening appointments on weekdays."

Price, guarantee and terms exactly as written in `04_OFFER.md`.

## The specificity ladder

Vague openers get ignored because they could be about anyone. Climb until the line could only be about this business and this reader.

1. Category: "Better bookkeeping for your business."
2. Audience: "Bookkeeping for construction companies."
3. Outcome: "Know which jobs make money."
4. Outcome with a time or number: "Profit per job, in your inbox by the 10th of every month."
5. Outcome with proof or a scene: "A roofer with 14 employees found two job types that lost money. He stopped bidding them."

To climb a rung, swap an adjective for a number from the proof or offer file, name a moment ("at 11 at night on a Sunday"), name the reader's role or what they use now (the spreadsheet, the patch kit), use their exact words from the language bank, or name the mechanism in a few words.

Stop rule: stop when the line gets long or the proof runs out. Rung 4 with no number beats rung 5 with an invented one. Most good hooks sit on rung 3 or 4, and rung 5 detail can go in the next line.

## Matching hooks to awareness level

"Dream customer" in `02_AUDIENCE.md` states the awareness level. Write for it, and give cold and warm audiences separate hooks.

| Level | Lead with | Hook types that fit | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | A symptom, scene or surprising point they recognize | Story opening, curiosity gap | Naming the product, jargon |
| Problem aware | The pain in their words | Pain call-out, mistake warning, how to promise | Leading with features |
| Solution aware | Why this approach beats what they tried | Contrarian claim, specific result | Explaining the problem at length |
| Product aware | Proof, the guarantee, the main objection answered | Specific result, direct offer | Introducing the business again |
| Most aware | The offer and the next step | Direct offer | A long build-up |

One roofing company, five levels:
- Unaware: "That brown spot on the ceiling started somewhere else."
- Problem aware: "A leak that comes back after every repair."
- Solution aware: "A patch fixes the spot. We find where the water gets in."
- Product aware: "[Guarantee from 04_OFFER.md, quoted exactly]. Free inspection, with photos."
- Most aware: "Book your free roof inspection. Photos in your inbox the same day."

## Headline and subheadline pairs

The headline stops the reader and makes the promise. The subheadline makes it specific and believable: who it is for, how it works, how fast, or what the reader does not have to do.

- The headline must work alone. The subheadline adds new information and never restates it.
- Together they answer: what do I get, is it for me, why should I believe it.
- An optional line above the headline can carry the audience call-out, freeing the headline for the outcome.

Four pairing patterns:
1. **Outcome, then how.** "Know your profit on every job." / "Monthly bookkeeping and job costing for construction companies, delivered by the 10th."
2. **Outcome, then without.** "Training that fits a week that never goes to plan." / "Three short sessions, rebuilt around your calendar every Sunday."
3. **Curiosity, then a hint of the payoff.** "The question we ask before quoting any roof." / "It decides whether you need a repair or a new roof. Free inspection, photos included."
4. **Pain, then relief.** "Stop doing the books on Sunday night." / "We handle bookkeeping, payroll and job costing for contractors, and walk you through the numbers every month."

Test: a stranger reading the pair can say what it is and who it is for.

## Video and on screen hooks

In short video the hook runs in three channels at once: what is said, what is shown and the text on screen. Assume the sound is off, so the visual and text must carry the hook alone.

- The first frame often serves as the thumbnail. No logo, no intro card, no "Hi, my name is". Start mid-action: a hand pulling down a wet ceiling tile, a calendar full of gaps, the founder already mid-sentence.
- Keep overlay text to a few words (under about 8), away from the edges where the platform puts captions and buttons. It sharpens the spoken hook and never contradicts it.
- Keep the spoken hook under about 3 seconds, usually under 12 words.
- Show the problem or the result. A contrast of two documents or two roofs works. Two bodies never do.

Example (scheduling software for clinics):
- Said: "This is what a double booking looks like at 9 in the morning."
- Shown: two patients at the front desk holding the same appointment card.
- Text: "Double booked. Again."

## Using the customer's own words

The "Language bank" in `02_AUDIENCE.md` is the best hook source in the folder. Customers use words no marketer would pick, and those words make a reader think "that's me".

- Read the whole bank first and mark quotes that are short, concrete and emotional. Use one as the hook, as it is or trimmed from the ends: "I keep bidding jobs blind."
- Match the category to a hook type. Pain quotes become pain call-outs. Desired outcome quotes become how to promises and headlines. Objection quotes become contrarian hooks ("Won't switching make a bigger mess?" becomes "Switching bookkeepers halfway through the year sounds messy. This is how we do it."). Trigger moment quotes become story openings. Success quotes become proof hooks only if the same quote is in `05_PROOF.md` with permission.
- Keep their vocabulary. If they say "the books", do not write "financial records". If you change their words, drop the quotation marks.
- A language bank quote shows how people talk. It is not proof.

If the bank is empty, use "Pains" and suggest the user adds real quotes.

## Writing variants and labeling angles

- Draft 10 or more openers and keep the best 3 to 5, or 15 to 20 for a hook bank.
- Label every variant with its angle. In a set of 3 to 5, no more than two share an angle. A hook bank uses at least 5 angles with no more than 4 hooks each.
- Hide the labels and read two variants side by side. If you cannot tell the angles apart, they are the same angle.
- For ads, every variant must fit the same body copy, so a test compares only openers. Star the top picks with one line on why.

## Testing hooks

- **Change one thing at a time.** Same image, body, offer, page and audience. Only the hook changes.
- **Test angles before wording.** Learn whether Pain or Proof works for this audience, then test lines within the winner.
- **Test big differences first, and be fair.** Near identical hooks teach little. Give each variant the same budget, audience and period, and call no winner after one day of small numbers.
- **Judge by the right signal.** For video, whether people watch past the first seconds. For static, cost per lead. Above both, leads that become customers. Cheap clicks that bring poor leads are not a win.
- **Log and reuse.** Record what won in the output file, then write new variants of the winning angle and try it as a page headline.

## Platform safe phrasing

Ad platforms reject ads that assert or imply a personal attribute of the viewer, and they restrict sensational promises. `06_RULES.md` and local law come first.

Personal attributes include health, body and weight, financial situation, age, ethnicity, religion or beliefs, sexual orientation, gender identity, disability and criminal record. Never pair "you" with any of them, directly or by implication.

- **Describe the solution, not the person.** "Are you drowning in debt?" becomes "A plan for paying off debt."
- **Talk about the plan, not the body.** "Hate how you look in photos?" becomes "A 25 minute training plan for busy weeks."

Keep out of openers: guaranteed results (unless `04_OFFER.md` has the guarantee, quoted exactly), cures or fast body changes, income promises, shock and fear ("This mistake could destroy your business"), fake urgency, and fake buttons or notifications in image ideas.

## Adapting to the language and market

- Write the hook natively in the language set in `01_BUSINESS.md`. A translated hook sounds translated, and idioms and puns rarely survive. Rebuild them or drop them.
- Directness varies by market. When unsure, undershoot, using the writing samples in `03_VOICE.md` as the guide.
- Question hooks sound natural in some languages and pushy in others. A statement is the safer default.
- Follow the address form in `03_VOICE.md`, and write numbers, dates and currency the local way.

## Common mistakes

- Opening with the company name or "Introducing".
- Clever over clear, so the reader has to decode the line.
- Vague words where a number or situation fits: "better results", "save time".
- Five variants that are one angle reworded.
- Writing for the wrong awareness level, or calling out a personal attribute.
- Curiosity with no payoff, or a promise the page does not keep.

## Preflight checklist

- [ ] The dream customer would feel it is about them, and it gives one reason to keep going.
- [ ] At least rung 3 on the ladder, with every number, result and name from `05_PROOF.md` or `04_OFFER.md`, or marked `[ADD PROOF: ...]`.
- [ ] Fits the awareness level in `02_AUDIENCE.md`, and uses the customer's words where they fit.
- [ ] Each variant is labeled, with no more than two per angle in a set of 3 to 5.
- [ ] No personal attribute, no guaranteed result, no fake urgency.
- [ ] For video, the spoken line, the visual and the text tell the same hook.
- [ ] The next line or page pays off the promise.
- [ ] Written natively in the business's language and voice, with no em or en dashes, no exclamation marks and none of the hype words in `.claude/skills/review/CHECKLIST.md`.
