# Messaging Psychology

**Use this file when:** you write any copy that has to persuade (hook, headline, ad, landing page, email, lead magnet) and need to decide what to lead with, which motive to speak to, and how to handle doubt.

**Covers:** buying motives, raw material, customer language, awareness levels, claim fatigue, objections, inertia, proof, guarantees, specificity, price framing, audience types, ethics, mistakes and a checklist.

## How to use this file

This file explains how buyers think. The business files say what is true, and they win. Raw material comes from `business/02_AUDIENCE.md`, promises from `04_OFFER.md`, results from `05_PROOF.md`. `06_RULES.md`, platform policies and local law override everything below. When a fact is missing, write `[ADD PROOF: ...]` or `[ANSWER NEEDED: ...]` and tell the user.

## Why people buy

People buy a change in their situation, not a product. Four motives drive almost every purchase. Most decisions mix them, but copy works best when it leads with one.

**Away from a problem.** Something hurts, costs money, wastes time or causes worry. Name it the way they live it, including the moment it shows up.
Example, a roofing company: "Every time it rains, you check the ceiling in the back bedroom."

**Toward an outcome.** They want something they do not have yet. Describe the after state in terms they could see or measure, not feeling words.
Example, an online fitness coach: "Finish the school fun run with your kids without stopping to walk."

**Status and identity.** People buy what fits who they are or want to be seen as. Show what kind of person makes this choice, without flattering or shaming.
Example, a B2B software tool: "For finance teams who are done explaining spreadsheet errors to the board."

**Safety and certainty.** Many buyers mainly want to avoid a mistake. This dominates when the price is high, the decision is hard to undo, or the buyer answers to someone else. Write to it with process and proof, not excitement.
Example, a dentist: "You get the full price in writing before we start."

Decision rule: read "Pains", "Dream outcome" and the "Language bank" together. Mostly complaints, lead with the problem. Mostly wishes, lead with the outcome. Many fears of a bad choice or "getting burned before", lead with certainty.

## Problem, outcome and circumstance

Collect three pieces of raw material before writing.

1. **Problem.** What is wrong now, in their words. From "Pains" and the Pain quotes.
2. **Outcome.** What is true after, specific enough to picture. From "Dream outcome" and the Desired outcome and Success quotes.
3. **Circumstance.** The situation that makes the problem bite: role, business size, season, trigger moment. From "Dream customer", "Best customer ever" and the Trigger moment quotes.

Circumstance makes copy feel written for one person. "Struggling with your books" could be anyone. "Doing the books at the kitchen table after the crew goes home" is someone specific.

Working sentence, for you and not the page: "For [circumstance], who [problem], this gets [outcome]." If the files cannot fill all three slots, ask the user one question before writing. Strong openings often pair two: "Third no-show this week, and it's only Wednesday."

## The customer's own words

Customers describe their problems in words a copywriter would never choose, and readers recognize themselves in those words.

- Use exact phrases from the "Language bank" in hooks, headlines, subject lines and FAQ questions.
- Prefer their plain words over industry terms, unless "Words we use" in `03_VOICE.md` shows they use the term too.
- Language bank quotes shape wording. They are not testimonials. Never display one as a customer quote unless it is also in `05_PROOF.md` with permission.
- Never invent a quote, a review or a "customer once told me" story.
- If the bank is thin, say so and suggest sources: call notes, customer emails, reviews.

"Automated reminders" is a feature. "Patients actually show up" is how the dentist says it.

## The five awareness levels

What the reader knows decides what you lead with and how long the copy runs. The level is in "Dream customer" in `02_AUDIENCE.md`. Cold ads usually reach less aware readers than an email to the list.

| Level | The reader | Lead with |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Does not see a problem, or thinks it is normal | A recognizable scene or a surprising observation about their world. Never the product |
| Problem aware | Feels the problem, does not know what fixes it | The problem in their words, what leaving it costs, then the type of solution |
| Solution aware | Knows the kind of fix, is comparing options | Why this approach is different, backed by proof |
| Product aware | Knows the offer, has not bought | Proof, offer details, answers to objections and risk |
| Most aware | Wants it, needs a reason to act now | The offer, the price if public, and the button near the top |

Rules of thumb:
- The less aware the reader, the later the product appears and the more copy comes before the ask. The more aware, the more you cut.
- For mixed traffic, write for the least aware group that will realistically arrive, and keep a button near the top for the rest.

Example, a bookkeeping firm. Problem aware: "You don't know if a job made money until April." Solution aware: "Most bookkeepers send one profit report for the whole company. We send profit per job." Most aware: "Book your free books review. It takes 20 minutes."

## When they have heard every claim before

In crowded markets the reader has heard every promise. When everyone says "grow your business", the phrase stops registering.

Signs: "What they have tried" is long, the language bank says "I've tried everything", or your headline could run on a competitor's page unchanged.

Never answer with a bigger promise. Use one of these, strongest first:

1. **A new mechanism.** Explain how this works and why it differs from what they tried, so they judge the method instead of the promise. It must be real, from "What makes us different" in `01_BUSINESS.md`. Example, a fitness coach: "Three short sessions a week, planned around your shifts, so work never costs you a week."
2. **More specificity.** Who, what, by when, how. "Profit per job by the 10th of every month" beats "better financial clarity".
3. **Stronger proof.** Let the result, the before and after or the exact quote from `05_PROOF.md` carry the claim. Without proof, a smaller promise plus a demonstration beats a big one.
4. **A narrower audience.** "For dental clinics with two to four chairs" reaches fewer people and convinces more of them.
5. **An honest contrarian point.** Say what is wrong with the common approach, only if the business really works differently. Never name a competitor.

Explain why past attempts failed without blaming the reader: "Generic plans assume you have free evenings."

## Objections

An objection is a reason not to buy that the reader believes is true. Answer with facts, not pressure. Take objections from "What holds them back", answers from `04_OFFER.md` and `05_PROOF.md`, and write `[ANSWER NEEDED: ...]` when no honest answer exists.

**"It won't work for me."** Their case feels different. Answer with proof from similar people, the conditions where it works, and honesty about who it is not for. Saying who it is not for makes the rest believable.

**"Not now."** Other things feel more urgent. Name what waiting costs in their terms, make the first step small, and mention real timing (a season, a scheduled price change). Never invent one.

**"Too expensive."** Usually the value is unclear, or they compare with the wrong thing. Make the outcome concrete, compare the price with the cost of the problem, and show what is included. If the price is truly too high for some, let them go. No discounts unless `04_OFFER.md` has one.

**"I don't trust you."** Answer with proof, credentials, a transparent process, a real guarantee if one exists, and a calm tone.

**"I can do it myself."** Sometimes true, so respect it. Show what doing it themselves really takes, point to what they already tried, and show what they get back by handing it over.

Answer the biggest objection before the main button on any selling page. Others go in the FAQ and in nurture emails, one per email. Do not argue. Acknowledge the doubt in their words ("Won't switching make a bigger mess?"), then answer it.

## Inertia and fear of change

The most common competitor is doing nothing. Change takes effort and carries risk, and the current problem is at least familiar.

- **Name the cost of staying put,** factually. "Another quarter of bidding jobs blind" is enough.
- **Shrink the first step.** Show exactly what happens after the click, from "Customer journey" in `04_OFFER.md`. "A 20 minute call, then a proposal within two days" is easier to start than "Get started".
- **Take the switching work off their plate.** If the business handles the move or setup, say so.
- **Use real timing only.** A clear next step does more than a fake countdown.

Answer the inertia line from "What holds them back" directly somewhere in the copy.

## Proof

All proof comes from `business/05_PROOF.md`. Types, roughly strongest first:

- **Results with a number, a source and a timeline.**
- **Case studies:** situation, what was done, what changed.
- **Testimonials,** word for word, with a name only if permission is yes.
- **Demonstration:** a sample report, a look inside the template, a walkthrough. Possible before any testimonials exist.
- **Volume and experience:** clients served, years, review scores, with source and date.
- **Credentials and process detail.** Weak alone, strong beside other proof.

Rules:
- **Put proof directly under the claim it supports,** not in a block at the bottom.
- **Match proof to the doubt.** "Won't work for me" needs someone similar. "Don't trust you" needs volume and credentials.
- **Specific beats impressive.** A modest exact result is more credible than a vague big one.
- **Never stretch proof.** No rounding up, merging clients or implying one result is typical.
- **Missing proof** becomes `[ADD PROOF: ...]`, or a smaller claim.

Example, a roofing company. Claim: "Most repairs are done in one visit." Under it, the review from the proof file that mentions one visit. Without that review, use a process fact: "We carry the common parts on the first visit."

## Risk reversal and guarantees

Risk reversal moves some of the buyer's risk to the seller. Honest, it is powerful. Dishonest, it is damaging.

- **Quote the guarantee exactly** from "Guarantee" in `04_OFFER.md`, conditions included. Never stronger.
- **If it says None, say nothing.** No "risk free", no "nothing to lose".
- **Lower risk with transparency instead:** what happens after the click, what it costs and when, what they keep if they stop.
- **Place it near the call to action,** where fear peaks.
- **Do not dress up a legal right** (a statutory return or cancellation right) as a special favor.

If a guarantee would help, suggest one to the user outside the copy (a service level promise, a redo promise, money back with conditions). It enters copy only once the owner adds it to `04_OFFER.md`.

## Specificity beats adjectives

Adjectives ask for trust. Specifics give a reason. Everyone claims "fast", "easy" and "high quality", so the words weigh nothing.

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| Fast turnaround | Proposal within 2 business days |
| Easy to use | Set up in one afternoon, no IT person needed |
| Personalized plan | Built around your shifts and your one free evening |
| Great results | [ADD PROOF: one client result with a number] |

Specifics come from the offer, the customer journey, the proof file and the process. They do not need numbers: "We call the day before and send the forms by text" is fully specific. Without a number in the files, use process detail, never an invented figure.

## Price against the cost of the problem

Alone on a page, a price gets compared with zero. Give the reader an honest comparison.

- **Compare with the cost of the problem,** and let the reader do the math with their own numbers (their last bad month, their weekly hours on the task).
- **Compare with realistic alternatives:** doing it themselves, hiring someone. Never name competitors or claim what they charge.
- **Break the price down only if billing works that way.** "From $890 a month" is fine when billed monthly. "Less than a coffee a day" for a one time fee is a gimmick.
- **Never invent a total value** by adding up made up prices for each part of the offer.
- **Follow price wording in `06_RULES.md`,** and show the price only if "Price" in `04_OFFER.md` says it is public.

Example, a bookkeeping firm: "Think about the last job that lost money. Then compare it with a month of bookkeeping." The reader supplies the number, so nothing is invented.

## Skeptical, affluent and aspirational audiences

"Dream customer", the price, and "Writing samples" in `03_VOICE.md` tell you which audience this is.

**Skeptical and affluent readers** have seen every pitch and fear wasted time more than the price. Give them:
- Understatement. Claim less than you could, and back it fully.
- Restraint. No hype words, no pressure, no capitals for emphasis.
- Evidence and process: how it works, who does it, what has been done before.
- Short copy, an easy next step, and a clear statement of who it is not for.

**Aspirational readers** are reaching for a new level: the first hire, the first race, the first fully booked month. Give them:
- A vivid, concrete picture of the after state.
- Identity and belonging: people like them who got there, and a group on the same path.
- Energy and encouragement. Energy is a tone, not a license to exaggerate.

**Adapt to the culture.** In some markets modest, dry copy reads as trustworthy and bold copy as a scam. In others confident copy is expected and modest copy reads as weak. The writing samples are the best signal. If they are missing, ask: "Should this sound more understated or more bold?" Copy moved between markets needs a new tone, not just a translation.

## Ethical lines

Never cross these lines:

- **No fake urgency or scarcity.** Deadlines and limits only if real and in the files. No resetting timers.
- **No shame.** Name the problem, not a flaw in the person. This also keeps ads clear of the personal attribute rule in `06_RULES.md`.
- **No exploiting vulnerable people.** Extra care with health, debt, grief, loneliness, addiction and children. Never amplify fear beyond the truth.
- **No implied promises** through "imagine" scenarios or exceptional stories presented as typical.
- **No invented proof,** composite customers or rounded up numbers.
- **No guilt on the way out.** "No thanks" is fine. "No, I like losing money" is not.
- **Regulated topics** (health, finance, income, legal) follow "Legal and industry rules" in `06_RULES.md` and local law.

If a request would cross a line, say so and offer an honest version that does the same job.

## Common mistakes

- Opening with the company before the reader feels understood.
- Writing for a most aware reader because the owner knows the product.
- Answering claim fatigue with a bigger claim.
- Features without what changes for the reader, and adjectives where a fact would do.
- Proof far from the claim it supports.
- Skipping the biggest objection.
- Hinting at "risk free" without a guarantee, or inventing a reason to act now.
- Carrying one market's tone into a market that punishes it.

## Checklist

- [ ] The copy leads with one clear motive.
- [ ] The opening uses at least two of problem, outcome and circumstance, from the business files.
- [ ] "Language bank" phrases appear in the hook, headline or FAQ.
- [ ] The lead matches the awareness level and the traffic source.
- [ ] Claim fatigue is met with mechanism, specificity, proof or a narrower audience.
- [ ] The biggest objection is answered before the main call to action, and the inertia line somewhere.
- [ ] Every result claim has proof beside it, or a placeholder.
- [ ] The guarantee is quoted exactly, or not mentioned.
- [ ] Adjectives are replaced by facts, and price framing invents nothing.
- [ ] The tone fits the audience and the market, and no ethical line is crossed.
