# Lead Magnets

**Use this file when:** you design, name or write a free resource that collects leads, write its opt-in page or delivery email, or judge whether an existing one works.

**Covers:** what a lead magnet is for, picking the problem, formats, naming, what goes inside, length, the opt-in page and welcome emails, filtering out non-buyers, delivery mechanics, judging results with the business's own numbers, common mistakes and a checklist.

## What a lead magnet is for

A lead magnet is a free, useful resource someone gets in exchange for their contact details. It has two jobs, and both matter more than the number of downloads.

1. **Attract the right buyers.** The people who want it should be the people who can buy the main offer. Crowds who will never buy are a cost, not an asset.
2. **Make the main offer the obvious next step.** After using it, the reader should think: "This helped, and the rest of my problem is exactly what they sell."

It also works as a sample. A clear, generous checklist suggests the paid work is clear and generous too. A thin, padded PDF suggests the opposite.

A lead magnet is not a summary of everything the business knows, a brochure or a discount. It is one useful thing that solves one problem.

## Picking the problem

The problem matters more than the format, design or title. A good candidate passes four tests.

1. **Narrow.** One person, one problem, one result. "Marketing for dentists" fails. "Cutting no-shows at a two chair clinic" passes.
2. **Felt now.** The reader already feels it and names it in their own words. Check "Pains" and the "Language bank" in `02_AUDIENCE.md`. A problem you think they should have does not count.
3. **Solved fully by the main offer.** The offer in `04_OFFER.md` solves this problem and the bigger one around it. An unrelated topic attracts the wrong people and builds no bridge.
4. **Quick win possible.** The reader gets one small, real result within a day: a fixed item, a clearer number, a decision, a draft.

Solve one slice of the problem completely and make the rest visible. Solve everything and nothing is left to buy. Solve nothing and nobody trusts the offer.

Good slices are usually one of these:
- **The first step** of the process the main offer runs, such as the month end checklist a bookkeeping firm uses on day one with every client.
- **A diagnosis** that shows where the reader stands. The main offer fixes what it finds.
- **The most common mistake,** and how to spot it.
- **A tool they need before buying,** such as questions to ask before signing, or a calculator showing what the problem costs.

Fill in this sentence before writing: "This helps [dream customer] [quick win], so they can [next result], which [main offer] delivers in full." If it feels forced, the problem is wrong.

If "Other offers" in `04_OFFER.md` already lists a free offer, start from it. If it fails a test, propose a narrower version next to it rather than replacing it.

## Formats and when each fits

Pick the format from the problem, not from what looks impressive.

| Format | Fits when | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Checklist | People skip steps or do them out of order | A roofer's storm damage checklist to run before calling the insurer |
| Cheat sheet | People look up the same facts again and again | A fitness coach's one page of portion sizes by hand measure |
| Template | People must write, plan or fill something in | A software tool's monthly cash forecast template |
| Swipe file | People need examples to copy and adapt | A dentist's five reminder texts that get patients to confirm |
| Short guide | A process needs some explanation | A bookkeeper's guide to pricing a job so it makes money |
| Scorecard or quiz | The reader wants to know where they stand | "Is your clinic ready for a second dentist?" with three result bands |
| Calculator | The problem is really a number | "What no-shows cost your clinic in a year" |
| Mini training | The method is easier to show than write | A short video walking through one technique |
| Sample or audit | Seeing the work on their own case is the best proof | A free review of one month of their books |

Notes:
- **Checklists, cheat sheets and templates** are quick to use and most likely to get used. They are the safe default.
- **Scorecards** suit offers sold on a call, because the result gives a reason to talk. Each result band says what to do next.
- **Calculators** need building. Write the spec: inputs, formula in plain words, outputs, one worked example marked as an example.
- **A mini training** needs the owner to record it. Write the outline or script.
- **An audit** costs the business time per lead. Use it only when the offer's price justifies that, and state exactly what is included.

The `/lead-magnet` skill writes checklists, cheat sheets, templates, swipe files, short guides, scorecard outlines and calculator specs in full. For a mini training or an audit, deliver the outline or script, the opt-in page brief and the delivery email.

## Naming

Most people decide from the title alone. The name sells the download, and the content keeps the promise.

Patterns that work:
- **Number plus outcome.** "The 7 point checklist before you send your first invoice."
- **The mistake.** "5 pricing mistakes that make good jobs lose money."
- **The tool, named by its job.** "The no-show cost calculator."
- **For one person.** "The first hire checklist for solo bookkeepers."
- **Before a moment.** "10 questions to ask before you sign a roofing contract."
- **Where do I stand.** "Is your clinic ready for a second chair? A 3 minute scorecard."
- **Speed, only when true.** "Plan your training week in 10 minutes."

Rules:
- Use the reader's words from the "Language bank". If they say "bidding jobs blind", that phrase belongs in the title or subtitle.
- Name a specific outcome, number of items or person. "The ultimate guide" names nothing.
- Promise only what is inside. If the title says 9 mistakes, there are 9.
- Clear beats clever. A pun the reader must decode loses to a plain description.
- The title carries the promise, the subtitle the how or the who: "The Job Profit Checklist. The 12 numbers to check before you bid your next job."
- Give title options different angles: Pain, Outcome, Curiosity, Proof, Contrarian, Identity, Mistake, Speed. Use Proof only if `05_PROOF.md` supports it.
- Match the market. Where understatement is trusted, a calm descriptive title beats a bold promise. Where boldness is expected, a confident outcome title fits. Follow `03_VOICE.md`.

Weak to strong:
- "Marketing Tips for Dentists" becomes "5 reminder texts that get patients to confirm."
- "Fitness Guide" becomes "The 20 minute hotel room workout for people who travel for work."
- "Free eBook: Financial Health" becomes "The month end checklist for contractors with 5 to 50 employees."

## What must be inside

Every lead magnet contains three things.

1. **The promised result.** Exactly what the title says, complete. Not a teaser that holds back half the list.
2. **A quick win,** placed early. Some readers only get through the first page, so it must pay off on its own.
3. **A natural next step.** At the end, name what the reader just fixed and what is still open, then give one call to action: "Main call to action" from `04_OFFER.md`, worded exactly the same.

Also include:
- **A short opening:** who it is for, what they get, how to use it. Two or three sentences.
- **The business's point of view:** the method, the order, the reason behind each step. This lifts it above a list anyone could find.
- **One labeled example:** a filled in template or a worked calculation. If it is not a real client story from `05_PROOF.md`, it says "Example".
- **Light branding:** the business name and a link on each page, so a forwarded copy still leads home.

Leave out padding, long founder stories, claims without proof, and hard selling before the content has delivered.

## Length and format

Consumable in under 10 minutes, whether read, filled in or watched.

- Checklists and cheat sheets fit on one or two pages.
- Templates explain every field and include one filled in example.
- A short guide stays at about 1,500 words or less, one action per section.
- A scorecard takes a few minutes and gives a result straight away.
- A mini training covers one technique in one sitting.

Make it work on a phone: short lines, readable text, skimmable headings. A clean document exported as a PDF is enough. For fill-in formats, offer an editable copy or suggest printing it. Short is not lazy. A one page checklist that took expertise to boil down beats a long guide that says everything.

## How it connects to the opt-in page and the welcome emails

The chain runs: ad or post, opt-in page, thank you page, delivery email, welcome emails, main call to action. Every link carries the same promise in the same words. If the ad says "the no-show calculator" and the page says "practice growth toolkit", trust drops.

**Opt-in page** (built with `/landing-page`):
- The headline names the lead magnet and its result.
- Three to five bullets, each pointing to something actually inside.
- A short form: email, maybe first name. Each extra field costs sign-ups, so add one only if it filters or routes leads.
- One line under the form on what arrives and when, and a short "this is for you if" list.

**Thank you page:** confirm it worked, name the sender to look for, give a direct backup link, and offer the main call to action for the few who are ready now.

**Welcome emails** (written with `/email`):
- The first delivers the link, one tip for using it today, and what comes next.
- The next help the reader get the quick win, answer the biggest objection from "What holds them back", share a result from `05_PROOF.md`, and bridge from the solved slice to the bigger problem.
- Early emails may ask for a small step, like opening the resource or replying. After that, each one points to the main call to action.

## Filtering out people who will never buy

A lead magnet that appeals to everyone attracts mostly people who will never buy. Make it appeal to buyers and bore everyone else.

- **Pick a topic only buyers care about.** A job costing checklist attracts construction owners. A generic budgeting guide attracts students.
- **Name the situation** in the title or subtitle. "For contractors with 5 to 50 employees" turns away the people "Positioning" in `01_BUSINESS.md` excludes.
- **Add "for you if" and "not for you if" lines** on the opt-in page.
- **Match the depth to the buyer.** Content that assumes the basics filters out people who are not ready.
- **Consider one qualifying question** on the form for high priced offers, such as company size. Fewer sign-ups, better ones. Ask the user first.
- **Say a paid offer exists,** in one honest line near the end or in the first email.
- **Avoid prizes and broad giveaways.** They draw people who want free things, not the offer.

Judge by buyers, not by list size.

## Delivery mechanics

Keep it simple and test the whole flow before going live.

1. **Make the file.** Export the finished content as a PDF from any document tool, or publish it as a web page. Include the business name and the call to action link.
2. **Host it** where it opens in one click, with no login or access request. For a shared file link, allow anyone with the link to view.
3. **Set up the email tool.** Connect the opt-in form and tag sign-ups by lead magnet, so welcome emails and reporting match.
4. **Send it instantly.** An automation sends the delivery email at sign-up. Later, people forget why they signed up.
5. **Back it up** with a direct link on the thank you page, in case the email lands in spam.
6. **Handle consent and privacy.** Rules on consent, confirmed opt-in and data storage differ by market. Follow `06_RULES.md`, link the privacy policy, and ask the user if the rules are unclear.
7. **Send from a person,** with a reply address someone reads.
8. **Test on a phone** with your own address: the email arrives, the link opens, the file reads well, the welcome emails start.
9. **Keep the link stable.** Replace updated files at the same link so older emails still work.

## Judging if it works

Judge by the business's own numbers over time, never by outside benchmarks. Every audience, offer and price differs, so someone else's "normal" says nothing about this one.

Track per lead magnet where possible: opt-in page visitors and sign-ups, delivery email opens and clicks, replies, calls booked or main calls to action taken, and customers, including how long they took to buy.

Compare with the business's earlier periods and its other lead magnets. Allow enough time and sign-ups before deciding, since a handful of leads proves nothing. Ask new customers which free resource they got first. Their answers often beat the numbers.

Read the pattern, then change one thing at a time:

| What you see | Likely cause | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| Few sign-ups, but they buy | Right topic, weak title or page | Test new titles and opt-in headlines |
| Many sign-ups, few calls or sales | Topic too broad, or no bridge | Narrow the problem, strengthen the next step and welcome emails |
| Sign-ups, few opens or clicks | Delivery or trust | Check that emails arrive, the sender name and the subject line |
| Few sign-ups, no sales | Problem not felt, or wrong audience | Go back to "Pains" and the "Language bank" |
| Calls booked, poor fit | Attracts people the business does not want | Add filtering: situation in the title, "not for you if", a qualifying question |

The real measure is customers who started with this lead magnet, set against what it cost to reach them. Fewer sign-ups and more buyers wins.

## Common mistakes

- A broad topic chosen for volume, attracting people who never buy.
- A problem the main offer does not touch.
- Giving away the whole method, or giving away nothing useful.
- A long ebook nobody finishes, when a one page checklist would get used.
- A clever title that hides what is inside, or promises more than it delivers.
- A quick win buried on page eight.
- No next step, or several competing ones.
- A different promise on the ad, the opt-in page and the email.
- A link that needs a login, or an email that arrives hours later.
- Invented results or "most people" claims added to look authoritative.
- Judging by downloads, or by someone else's benchmark.

## Checklist

- [ ] The problem is narrow, felt now, solved fully by the main offer, and allows a quick win.
- [ ] The bridge sentence reads naturally.
- [ ] The format fits the problem, and you can say why in one sentence.
- [ ] The title is specific, uses the reader's words, and promises only what is inside.
- [ ] The content delivers the full promise, every number included, with a quick win early.
- [ ] It takes under 10 minutes to read, fill in or watch.
- [ ] It ends with what was fixed, what is open, and the main call to action worded as in `04_OFFER.md`.
- [ ] Every example not from `05_PROOF.md` is labeled as an example.
- [ ] Opt-in page, thank you page and emails carry the same promise and call to action.
- [ ] The topic and wording filter out the people "Positioning" excludes.
- [ ] Delivery is instant, needs no login, and has been tested on a phone.
- [ ] It will be judged on sign-ups, calls and customers from this lead magnet, over time.
