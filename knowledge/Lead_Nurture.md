# Lead Nurture

**Use this file when:** you write a welcome sequence, nurture or newsletter emails, or a re-engagement sequence, or plan how a new lead gets from signup to the main call to action.
**Covers:** the first email, the welcome sequence, email types, subject lines and preview text, plain text style, replies, the ask, cadence, segments, re-engagement, deliverability, consent, mistakes and a checklist.

## What nurture is for

A new lead has raised a hand. They want what they signed up for and they are curious, but they do not trust the business yet. Nurture has two jobs:

1. **Build trust.** The reader comes to believe the business understands the problem, tells the truth and does good work.
2. **Show the path.** The reader sees the steps from where they are now to the "Dream outcome" in `02_AUDIENCE.md`, and sees the main call to action as the sensible next step.

Decision rule: every email leaves the reader more helped, more sure, or one clear step further along. If it does none of these, cut it.

## Where the material comes from

Never invent the substance of an email. Pull it from the business files:

- Pains and phrases: "Pains" and "Language bank" in `02_AUDIENCE.md`.
- Wrong beliefs: "What they have tried". Objections: "What holds them back".
- Stories: "Origin story" in `01_BUSINESS.md` and "Best customer ever" in `02_AUDIENCE.md`.
- Results, numbers and quotes: `05_PROOF.md` only.
- The ask: "Main call to action" in `04_OFFER.md`. A smaller step: "Other offers". What happens after the click: "Customer journey".
- Promises: "Guarantee" in `04_OFFER.md`, quoted exactly, or nothing.

When an email needs proof that does not exist, write `[ADD PROOF: what is needed]`. A clear explanation of how the work is done is a fair stand-in for a missing result. A made-up result never is.

## The first email

Send it the moment someone signs up. It is the one email people are actively looking for. Its job is to deliver what was promised and set expectations, in under 120 words:

- **The thing they asked for**, linked in the first two lines.
- **Where to start.** One page or one step to do first. A freebie that gets used builds trust.
- **What comes next.** How many emails, how often, and what they bring.
- **One reply question**, easy to answer in a line.

The subject line is plain, because the reader is searching for it. "Your job profit checklist" beats a clever line here.

Example, an online fitness coach:

> Subject: Your 3 day meal plan is here
>
> Here it is: [LINK]
>
> Start with the shopping list on page 2. Do that today and the other days get easy.
>
> Over the next week I'll send you a few short emails about eating well when work runs late.
>
> Quick question: which meal do you skip most often? Hit reply and tell me.
>
> [Sender name]

## The welcome sequence

Default: 5 emails over 7 days, counted from signup.

| Day | Job | Call to action |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Deliver the freebie, say where to start and what comes next | Open the download, reply |
| 1 | Quick win: one step from the freebie they can do today | Do the step, reply |
| 3 | A story showing why what they have tried fails, soft mention of the offer | Main call to action, one calm line |
| 5 | One result from `05_PROOF.md`, the biggest objection answered | Main call to action |
| 7 | A clear invitation, what happens after the click, the guarantee if real | Main call to action |

- **Quick win.** The smallest action in the freebie with a visible payoff, done in one sitting. A small result gained with your help is the best reason to keep reading.
- **Story.** From "Origin story" or "Best customer ever". Show the old way failing in concrete detail, then the moment the better way became clear. A final line says the offer exists for people in the same spot.
- **Proof and objection.** Name the objection from "What holds them back" in the reader's own words, answer it honestly, and back the answer with one proof item. If proof is thin, explain how the process prevents what they fear.
- **Invitation.** What you are inviting them to, who it fits and who it does not, step 1 of "Customer journey", the guarantee quoted exactly if one exists, and permission to say no.

The ask grows. Emails 1 and 2 ask for small actions. From email 3 the main call to action appears, worded exactly as in `04_OFFER.md` every time. When the sequence ends, move the reader into regular nurture so they do not drop into silence.

For a high price or a long decision (B2B software, a large home project), add proof and objection emails or space them out. When the user asks for a different plan, follow it.

## One idea, one job

Each email has one idea and one job: deliver, teach, prove, answer an objection, or invite. Write the job down first.

- Can you state the point in one sentence without "and"? If not, it is two emails.
- Cut paragraphs that do not serve the job, even good ones. Save them for another email.
- One call to action. The link may appear twice, but always to the same place.

A shape for most value emails: pay off the subject line in the first two lines, give the idea with one concrete detail, connect it to the reader's week ("If you bid a job this week, check this first"), then ask.

## Email types and when to use each

Rotate types so no type comes twice in a row. In a run of four emails, use four different types.

| Type | What it is | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Lesson | One how-to step the reader can use without buying | Quick wins, problem aware readers |
| Story | A real event from the founder or a customer | Building trust, showing why the old way fails |
| Case | Before, what changed, and the result, from `05_PROOF.md` | Product aware readers, right before an offer |
| Myth | A common wrong belief and what is true instead | Readers stuck in a wrong approach |
| Behind the scenes | How the work is done, a decision, a lesson learned | Lowering the fear of what working together is like |
| Question | One short question | Restarting replies, learning, sorting by interest |
| Offer | The invitation is the whole email | Only after real value has been given |

Examples: a bookkeeping firm's lesson on checking whether last month's jobs made money, or a dentist's behind the scenes look at a first visit.

Watch out: teach one step well, not seven badly. Stories and cases come only from the business files or the user, never invented or rounded up. An offer email states the price exactly as in `04_OFFER.md` if it is public.

## Subject lines and preview text

The subject line makes opening easy and honest, and the first two lines of the body pay it off. If they do not, readers learn to ignore you.

- Short enough for a phone. Under 50 characters is a good default. In languages with long words, put the key words first.
- Specific beats clever.
- Honest curiosity points at something real in the email. Bait promises what the email does not deliver.

Examples by angle: (Benefit) Know which jobs made money. (Curiosity) The line we check first on every invoice. (Pain) "I keep bidding jobs blind". (Story) The week our biggest client almost left. (Proof) How [client] got [result from 05_PROOF.md]. (Direct) Your checklist, plus where to start. (Contrarian) Why we tell clients to eat more at lunch.

Never use "Re:" or "Fwd:" on a new email, urgency words unless the deadline is real, ALL CAPS, bait like "You won't believe this", or fake personal lines like "Sorry I missed you".

**Preview text** shows next to or under the subject line. Always set it, or the tool pulls the first text it finds, often a greeting or a "view in browser" link. It adds to the subject line, never repeats it, and runs 35 to 90 characters. Example: subject "Know which jobs made money", preview "A check you can do on Friday afternoon".

Write three subject lines per email with three different angles, and make sure the opening lines pay off all three.

## Plain text and writing like a person

- **From name.** A real person, or the person plus the company if the voice calls for it. Replies reach someone who reads them.
- **Plain text.** No templates, banners or image stacks, and few links.
- **One reader.** "You", singular, never "Hi everyone". Follow "How we address the reader" in `03_VOICE.md`. How formal a greeting should be differs between languages and markets.
- **Short paragraphs**, one to three sentences. Most people read on a phone.
- **Concrete over polished.** "The invoice was on the dashboard of the truck" beats "our process was disorganized".
- **Sign with a name.** Add a P.S. only when it adds something. Use first name fields only if the data is clean.
- **Read it aloud** next to the "Writing samples" in `03_VOICE.md`. If it sounds like a press release, rewrite it.

## Asking for replies

Replies tell you what readers care about, bring new phrases in their own words, and tend to help emails reach the inbox. Ask one easy question at the end, as the email's call to action: "Which job type are you least sure makes money?" Or offer a choice: "Reply 1 if you do your own books, 2 if someone else does."

Someone has to answer the replies, so tell the user. When a reply holds a strong phrase, offer to add it to the "Language bank". When it holds a result, offer to add it to `05_PROOF.md` once the customer agrees. Never change business files without asking.

## Asking for the sale without pressure

A clear invitation is a service. People who never hear how to get help stay stuck.

- **Give first**, and let the ask grow through the sequence.
- **Say what happens next.** Describe the first step of "Customer journey". For a call, say how long it is, who they talk to, and what they leave with.
- **Same words every time** for the main call to action.
- **Soft or direct.** A soft ask is one calm line at the end of a value email. A direct ask is an offer email. In ongoing nurture, most emails carry a soft ask or a reply question, and offer emails come now and then, after value.
- **Permission to say no.** "If now isn't the right time, that's fine. Next week's email is about [topic] either way."
- **Real urgency only**, as written in `04_OFFER.md` or given by the user.

Never use guilt, shame about the reader's situation, countdowns that reset, a "last chance" that is not the last, or hints that they are failing because they have not bought.

## Cadence

Consistency beats frequency. A reader who hears from you on the same day each week remembers you.

- **Welcome:** 5 emails over 7 days.
- **Ongoing nurture:** one email a week by default. Twice a week when there is enough real material to keep every email useful.
- **Timing:** a time that fits the audience in `02_AUDIENCE.md`, in the reader's time zone, as a starting point to test. Check local holidays and weekend habits.
- **Launches** can carry more emails if readers are told at the start what to expect.
- **No long silence followed by a pitch.** Run re-engagement first.

Judge the rhythm against the list's own history, such as rising unsubscribes or replies saying "too many".

## Segmenting by behavior

Segment by what people did, not by guesses about who they are. Trust clicks and replies more than opens, because some email apps open messages automatically.

- **New:** the welcome sequence only.
- **Clicked the offer, did not act:** the biggest objection answered, or a case.
- **Replied:** a personal answer. Tag the topic they raised.
- **Booked:** no sales emails. Confirmation, reminders and how to prepare.
- **Bought:** no sales emails for that offer. Onboarding, then ask about their result.
- **Cold:** re-engagement.

Never pitch an offer to someone who already bought it, and stop the booking sequence the moment someone books. Keep segments few, and only build the ones the user will maintain.

## Re-engagement and removing inactive subscribers

Cold means no clicks or replies for a period agreed with the user, for example three to six months on a weekly list. Default: 3 emails over 7 days.

1. **Day 0:** an honest check in that admits the silence, the most useful thing the business has, and one click to stay subscribed.
2. **Day 3:** the best value email you can write, ending with the main call to action.
3. **Day 7:** a short last email. Mention removal only if the user confirmed it.

Subject line examples: "Still want these emails?" and, only when removal is confirmed, "Last one from me unless you click".

Afterwards, remove or suppress those who did not click. Mailing people who ignore you can hurt how inbox providers treat everything else you send. For a long quiet list, send to a small part first and remove bounces before sending to the rest.

## Deliverability basics

- **Authenticate the sender.** Add the sender records your email tool asks for to your domain (often called SPF, DKIM and DMARC), and send from your own domain, not a free mailbox.
- **Send steadily.** A new domain or a long quiet list starts with small sends and grows.
- **Keep the list clean.** Remove hard bounces and, after re-engagement, cold subscribers. Use confirmed opt-in where the market expects it.
- **Never buy, rent or scrape lists.** If the user mentions one, stop and tell them not to send to it.
- **Make leaving easy.** An unsubscribe is better than a spam complaint.

Never promise a specific inbox result. Explain what improves the odds.

## Consent, unsubscribe and local rules

Email law, consent rules and required sender details differ by country. Do not state legal specifics. Follow `business/06_RULES.md` and the user's local rules, and if neither answers the question, tell the user to check before sending.

- Only email people who agreed to hear from the business. Treat a signup as agreement to what the form promised, and nothing more. If people would not remember signing up, check the rules before sending anything.
- Every email has a working unsubscribe link and the sender details the market requires. The email tool usually adds them, so remind the user to check they are on.
- Never trick people out of unsubscribing or hide the link.
- Write in the language from `01_BUSINESS.md`. How direct an ask can be differs by culture, so match the "Tone" and "Writing samples" in `03_VOICE.md`.

## Common mistakes

- A delivery email that buries the link under a long welcome.
- Pitching before the reader has opened the freebie.
- Several ideas in one email, so none of them lands.
- Subject lines the body never pays off.
- Invented results, or a guarantee made stronger than `04_OFFER.md`.
- Fake deadlines and "last chance" emails that are not the last.
- Sales emails to people who already booked or bought.

## Checklist

- [ ] Delivery email: freebie linked in the first two lines, where to start, under 120 words.
- [ ] One idea, one written job and one call to action per email, and types rotate.
- [ ] Three subject lines with three different angles, all paid off in the opening lines.
- [ ] Preview text is set, 35 to 90 characters, and adds something new.
- [ ] Plain text, written to one reader, short paragraphs, signed by a person.
- [ ] Stories from the business files or the user, results only from `05_PROOF.md`.
- [ ] From email 3 on, the main call to action matches `04_OFFER.md` word for word.
- [ ] No fake urgency. The guarantee is quoted exactly or left out.
- [ ] People who booked or bought are excluded from sales emails for that offer.
- [ ] Removal is mentioned only if the user confirmed it.
- [ ] Consent, unsubscribe and sender details follow `06_RULES.md` and local rules.
